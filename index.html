The physics inside

Each ball is a sphere in free fall through still air. Taking downward as positive, its acceleration is

a = g·(1 − ρV/m) − (ρ·Cd·A / 2m) · v·|v|

The first term is gravity minus buoyancy (V = ball volume, ρ = air density); the second is quadratic aerodynamic drag on the cross-section A = πr². Drag grows with the square of speed, so each ball approaches a terminal velocity vt = √(2·(m − ρV)·g / (ρ·Cd·A)) where drag balances weight.

The tennis ball (≈58 g over a 6.7 cm circle, Cd ≈ 0.55 thanks to its fuzz) carries about ten times more weight per unit of drag area than a 5 g crumpled sheet of A4 — so the paper ball reaches its terminal velocity (≈6–7 m/s) within a couple of metres and falls behind, while the tennis ball keeps accelerating toward ≈22 m/s. Set the air density to zero (or untick air resistance) and both fall identically: mass cancels out of a = g.

The equations are integrated with a fixed-step 4th-order Runge–Kutta scheme (480 steps per simulated second). Landings are located by interpolation inside the step; bounces reverse the velocity scaled by the restitution coefficient. Crumpled paper is aerodynamically messy — its measured Cd ranges roughly 0.5–1.1 depending on how tightly it is crushed, which is exactly why it is a slider.
